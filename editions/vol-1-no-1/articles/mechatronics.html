A Conversation with Mechatronics Head of Programme

By Farkas Angelus · Vol. 1, No. 1

I had the privilege of sitting down with Andrei-Alexandru Popa, the head of mechatronics. We talked about his journey here and his vision of what he would like mechatronics to be.

Andrei-Alexandru Popa finished his bachelor and master in mechatronics before quickly starting work and teaching at the university while also completing his PhD in additive manufacturing. He has been teaching mechanical design and machine components to mechanical and mechatronics students, but last year he became head of program for mechatronics after Lars Duggen. Since then, he has brought a new perspective to what mechatronics should be and what it should mean to study at SDU.

“So, to me mechatronics is better defined by the language that needs to be spoken, very fluently, between mechanics, electronics, software programming.”

— Andrei-Alexandru Popa, head of mechatronics

He explained that mechatronics is not simply a mix of mechanics, electronics, and software, but a way of thinking across disciplines. In his view, it is closer to systems engineering than to any one of the individual fields it draws from. This means students are not trained to work only within a narrow technical niche, but to understand how different components interact within a whole product.

His emphasis on autonomy also shaped the way he talked about the discipline. He explained that autonomy is not limited to vehicles or robots in the usual sense, but also applies to manufacturing methods and other mechatronic devices. He does not want mechatronics to be identified only with robotics, drones, or other trendy subfields. Instead, he sees it as the intelligent and autonomous application of technology wherever it makes sense, which he argued is happening in far more areas today than it did just five years ago.

He summarised the field in two core concepts: the language between disciplines and the role of autonomy. He also stressed that robotics is a subfield of mechatronics, even though it has its own identity. That perspective is especially relevant at SDU, where much of the research focus is on autonomous drones and underwater drones. Yet he pointed out that any robot, including a 3D printer, is also a mechatronic product.

When asked what advantage a student gains by studying mechatronics rather than focusing on a single child discipline, Popa returned to the idea of systems engineering. A mechatronics graduate can go on to work in software, for example, but they will carry a broader mindset. They learn not to think about their own part in isolation, but in relation to the whole product.

A central part of his role as head of program is ensuring that students receive the quality of education they need to develop that mindset. He described his job in a simple but telling way: quality. For him, the purpose of the program is not only to teach technical knowledge, but to cultivate the way students think, collaborate, and solve problems across disciplines.
