Student Launches Website Listing Every Student Housing Option in Sønderborg

By Ariana Kalsbeek · Vol. 1, No. 1

When Andrea Bortolotti arrived in Sønderborg, he was number 200 on the waitlist for Alssund Kollegiet. He had no way of knowing how long that would take—whether the queue moved fast or slow, whether he’d have an offer by Christmas or still be waiting in March. The Studiebolig Syd website didn’t say. There was no queue velocity, no estimated wait time, nothing that let him plan around the uncertainty.

He wasn’t alone. Every September, hundreds of students arrive in Sønderborg to find that the city’s housing infrastructure hasn’t kept up with how much the university has grown. Waitlists stretch for months. Students end up in temporary housing or commuting from Augustenborg or Nordborg, while they wait for something better. “Half the website was in English and half was in Danish,” Andrea says. “I didn’t know how fast the queue was moving or how long it would take.”

Other Danish student cities already had what Sønderborg was missing. In Aarhus, for example, the housing platform puts prices, vacancies, and a map on the same screen and shows how long queues typically take to move. Students can plan. Nothing like that existed for Sønderborg. So over his winter break, Andrea started building one.

“I didn't know how fast the queue was moving or how long it would take.”

— Andrea Bortolotti, Electronics Engineering student

Studenthousingsonderborg.app launched publicly in April. It covers every dorm managed by Studiebolig Syd, and also youth housing from associations like B42 and Søbo that most incoming students never find simply because there’s no single place to look. Everything is searchable together—vacancy rates, pricing, floor plans, amenities—in one view.

Andrea’s website includes an AI tool that reads floor plan images to calculate the real private area, so the price per square metre you see on the website reflects what you’re actually getting. The square metre figure listed by Studiebolig Syd for each apartment includes shared kitchens and common areas, not just your private room. The actual size of your own space is smaller than advertised—something many students only realise after they’ve already signed.

For students weighing housing outside the city, public transport data is built in: exact commute times, bus routes, and estimated cost of monthly transport. A classmate of Andrea’s who lives outside Sønderborg sometimes has to leave campus at four in the afternoon to catch the last bus home. On Andrea’s website, that kind of trade-off is visible before you sign anything.

The map on studenthousingsonderborg.app shows every dorm as a circle sized by number of rooms. Image: Andrea Bortolotti / studenthousingsonderborg.app

“The feature I’m most proud of is the map,” Andrea says. Each dorm is shown as a circle, sized by number of rooms, so you can read the city’s student geography at a glance. Supermarkets with opening hours, gyms, and parks are layered in. Images have AI-generated alt text and the site can be navigated fully by keyboard. It was built to be useful and accessible to as many people as possible.

The platform got its validation in June, when Studiebolig Syd launched their new website. Links had changed, things were harder to find than before, and students searching for housing information found their way to Andrea’s page instead. Earlier that year, Andrea had gone to the Studiebolig Syd office to explore whether his platform could become part of their official offering—or at least whether the survey data he’d collected on student satisfaction with the housing search might be useful to them. They weren’t interested; they had just contracted an agency to redo their site. “I think I might have spooked them,” he says. The survey found that most students considered the Studiebolig Syd site at least somewhat difficult to use.

What comes next depends on other people showing up. The platform is heavy on data and AI—floor plans read by algorithms, descriptions generated from scraped text, alt text written by a model. Andrea wants to change that balance. “There is a lot of AI, but I want it to be more human,” he says. That means verified resident reviews from students who have actually lived in each dorm, and a blog covering things incoming students might need to know, from finding second-hand furniture to the mechanics of Danish bureaucracy. “It would be really great to have different students working on it,” he says. “But I don’t really know how to ask them.” For now the platform runs on one person’s time, but Andrea is very much open to collaboration.

Meanwhile, the municipality is working on longer-term solutions. The stopgap measures are already visible: the container housing between UK and SK, the conversion of hotel 6400 into student housing, and since 2025 the old youth hostel near Bilka has been repurposed into 60 student apartments. These have bought time, but not solved the underlying shortage. Last September, plans were approved for around 550 new student apartments and 32 public housing units on a former materials yard close to the existing dorms on Skovvej. In total, the municipality is aiming to have 2,500 new homes by 2030, with many of those being youth housing.
